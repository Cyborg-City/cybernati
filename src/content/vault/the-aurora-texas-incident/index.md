# Aurora, Texas (1897): The One They Can't Explain Away

If you know one UFO crash story, it's Roswell. Flying saucer, cover-up, secret base, the whole deal.

But what if I told you there's a case that's harder to debunk than Roswell — and it happened 50 years earlier?

Roswell skeptics have an easy out: "It was just a weather balloon / spy balloon / test dummy." You can argue about it forever.

But Aurora, Texas? That one makes the skeptics squirm.

Because here's the thing. If you claim people in 1897 saw an airplane, a weather balloon, or a secret military test craft — none of those things existed yet. The Wright brothers wouldn't fly for another six years. There were no aircraft. No spy balloons. No jets. And yet on April 17, 1897, something crashed on a judge's property in a tiny Texas town, and the pieces were real enough that people are still arguing about it 130 years later.

So what actually happened?

---

## The Incident

An article published in the *Dallas Morning News* on **April 19, 1897** reported that two days earlier, early risers in Aurora, Texas saw a large, silver, cigar-shaped object drifting low over the town. It struck a tower on the property of **Judge J. S. Proctor**, exploded with a tremendous blast, and scattered debris over several acres.

**The timeline:**

- **November 1896** — The "Great Airship Mystery" begins. Thousands of people in California report seeing a large, cigar-shaped craft with bright lights moving through the night sky. Sightings spread east over the next six months.
- **April 17, 1897** — A silver cigar-shaped object crashes on Judge Proctor's farm in Aurora, Texas, striking a tower and exploding. Debris scatters over the property.
- **April 19, 1897** — The *Dallas Morning News* publishes S.E. Haydon's article describing the crash. The pilot is described as "not of this world."
- **April 19, 1897** — The same edition of the paper contains 15 other stories about airship sightings across Texas, Oklahoma, and Kansas — the Aurora story wasn't unique that day.
- **1897 (same day)** — The pilot is given a Christian burial in an [[posts/theres-an-alien-grave-in-texas-and-nobody-can-dig-it-up|unmarked grave]] at the Aurora Cemetery. Wreckage is thrown down Proctor's well.
- **~1935–1945** — **Brawley Oates** buys Proctor's property. He cleans debris out of the well to use it for water.
- **~1945** — Oates develops an extremely severe case of rheumatoid arthritis. He believes the well water — contaminated by the crash debris — is the cause. He seals the well with concrete.
- **1973** — **Bill Case** (aviation writer, *Dallas Times Herald*) and **Jim Marrs** (reporter, *Fort Worth Star-Telegram*) re-investigate the story. They find a small grave with an unusual marker at the Aurora Cemetery. A metal detector reveals three large pieces of metal inside.
- **1973–74** — The gravestone is stolen. When Case and Marrs return, the metal detector shows nothing. Three drill holes mark where the metal used to be.
- **2005** — *UFO Files* airs "Texas' Roswell," featuring interviews with eyewitnesses and the MUFON investigation.
- **2008** — *UFO Hunters* unseals the well for testing. Water shows elevated aluminum levels. The base of a windmill derrick is found, confirming a tower existed at the site.
- **2008** — Ground-penetrating radar confirms a short grave at the cemetery — child-sized, consistent with the pilot's described stature.

The pilot was described as **small** — the grave was clearly not full-sized, which matched the reports. The burial was Christian, despite the pilot being "not of this world." Whatever was in that grave, it was buried with dignity.

## The Evidence That Still Exists

Here's what's actually been found — not stories, not claims, but documented physical evidence:

**Pieces of unusual metal.** Fragments from the crash site were analyzed. One fragment, tested by **Dr. Tom Gray**, was 75% iron and 25% zinc — an unusual composition — and remarkably, it showed **no magnetic properties** despite being mostly iron. Another fragment analyzed by **John Schuessler** at an aerospace lab was 95% pure aluminum with 5% iron — an alloy that "doesn't mix that way" by normal metallurgy, and would have been impossible to produce in a 19th-century farm town. At the time, aluminum was more expensive than gold.

**A short grave confirmed by ground-penetrating radar.** The cemetery association wouldn't allow exhumation, but radar scans showed ground disturbance consistent with a child-sized grave in the exact area where the marker had been.

**The base of the tower.** Debunkers long argued the story was impossible because Judge Proctor had no windmill. But investigators found the stone and metal footings of a derrick structure at the well site. There was a tower. The story was never about a windmill — it was about a wooden derrick used to haul water.

**The sealed well.** It's still there under a concrete slab. The water tested positive for elevated aluminum when it was sampled in 2008. No large debris was found, but it's unclear how thoroughly the well was searched or whether previous owners removed material.

## What We Know and What We Don't

Every source, from the most enthusiastic believer to the most skeptical debunker, agrees on the core facts:

- An article was published in a major newspaper.
- A crash was reported involving a large cigar-shaped object.
- A pilot described as "not of this world" was buried in the town cemetery.
- Wreckage was thrown down a well.
- A man later bought the property, cleaned the well, and developed severe health problems.
- In 1973, investigators found an unusual grave with metal readings that later vanished.
- The tower that the object struck — the one debunkers said never existed — did exist. Its footings are still there.

What nobody agrees on is what it means.

Was it a hoax? **Etta Pegues**, an 86-year-old Aurora resident, told *Time* magazine in 1980 that Haydon made the whole thing up as a joke to put Aurora on the map after the railroad bypassed the dying town. It's a plausible story — the town had been devastated by a boll weevil infestation, a fire, and a spotted fever epidemic. Maybe they needed the attention.

But if it was a hoax, who planted the metal fragments? Who drilled the holes in the grave to extract the metal after the headstone was stolen? Why did **James Ewell** — son of the city marshal who guarded the cemetery — tell researchers that his grandfather helped bury the pilot and his father "knew who took the headstone but could do nothing about it"? That sounds like someone implicating the government, not like a practical joke that got out of hand.

## What If It's Real?

If the Aurora crash was real, it changes the timeline on everything.

Roswell (1947) is usually cited as the beginning of the modern UFO era. But Aurora pushes that back by 50 years — to before the invention of aircraft. It means something was flying over the United States in 1897 that no one could explain, and when it crashed, the response was the same as it would be today: secrecy, missing evidence, and a town divided between those who saw it and those who wished it would go away.

The well is still there. The grave is still there. The footings of the tower are still there. The technology to analyze the well water and the aluminum fragments is better now than it was in 2008.

At some point, someone's going to take a serious look at what's actually in that well. And when they do, the Aurora case might finally stop being a footnote in UFO history and start being the thing that makes everyone reconsider how long this has really been going on.
